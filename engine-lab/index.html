UNDER THE FLOORBOARDS

The Engine Lab

No off-the-shelf engine hums under this studio's games. The machinery is written by hand, in C, by the people who have to answer for it — which makes the lab less a department and more a promise: if it moves, somebody here can explain exactly why.

Built, not bought

AirportSim runs on a hand-rolled engine: its world — aircraft, contracts, weather, money, memory — is simulated in plain C, kept deliberately separate from the machinery that draws it. That separation is the lab's favourite trick. The world can be exercised headlessly, thousands of times, by test batteries that never open a window.

The SDL3 standard

LONGSHOT set the studio's modern foundation: C11 on SDL3, pinned and carried with the project. Tonight AirportSim is being moved onto that same foundation, retiring its SDL2 underpinnings. Two living games, one base — every lesson learned on either bench then belongs to both.

Same seed, same world

Randomness in the lab is a tool with a serial number. Worlds are dealt from seeds, so any scene a photograph captures can be dealt again, exactly, for the re-test — and a guard exists to prove the dealing hasn't drifted. Chance is welcome in the games; it is not welcome in the evidence.

The bench that checked itself

LONGSHOT's design bench was validated the hard way: its outputs were compared against twelve published values from the real literature of its subject, and it reproduced all twelve. That is the lab's idea of a reference shelf — don't trust the instrument until the instrument has been caught being right.

An engine you wrote yourself can still surprise you — but it can never hide from you.

Currently on the bench

One engine transplant, in progress tonight: AirportSim's move from SDL2 to SDL3, carried out in slices small enough to prove individually, with the full test battery and the proof camera attending every step. The patient is expected not merely to survive but to come off the table standing on the studio's standard foundation.
