The studio

Mission, philosophy, and how AI-assisted engineering is actually run here.

Mission

Build real games — fun, detailed, and complex underneath a simple surface — with AI developers doing the engineering and a human owner making the decisions that matter. The studio's products are the games; its method is the proof that the games are what we say they are.

Development philosophy

Evidence, or it didn't happen

Every "done" report quotes its evidence: the test counts on the exact commit, the gate's exit code, the checksum. A successful compile is evidence the code compiles — nothing more. Claims are graded on a ladder — Implemented (the code exists), Demonstrated (it ran and behaved), Established (it held up across varied conditions) — and the grade is never upgraded for presentation.

Records outrank websites

Each project is run from its own records: a releases registry, a changelog, a project-state file, architecture and test documents. This website is a public echo of those records. If the site and a record disagree, the record is right and the site gets corrected.

Small, reversible, verified steps

Work proceeds in milestones with explicit gates: a plan is reviewed before implementation, results are reported with their evidence, and the owner accepts each phase before the next begins. Failure is reported with its evidence preserved — a failed experiment is a result, not an embarrassment to be quietly tuned away.

Held is a first-class status

A finished build is not a release. Blacksite 1.5.0 and the WorldForge 1.0.0 candidate are both built, recorded, and held — their registries say so in plain words, and a local tag is never treated as a publication. Statuses change on evidence and owner decisions, not on the calendar.

The shared foundation

Every Section Zero game is built the same way at the foundation level: C11 throughout, with SDL2 in Blacksite, AirportSim and WorldForge — the newest game, LONGSHOT, is being built on SDL3, by the owner's decision; 100% procedural assets (no loaded art, sound, or music files); the shared Section Zero studio card opening every game; a written project brain (state, architecture, roadmap, tests, decisions) kept beside the code; and the same release bar for a small game as for a large one. What the games do not share is an engine codebase — that finding, and what it does and does not establish, is recorded in the Engine Laboratory.

The studio philosophy

The studio's philosophy is a written document, established by the owner on 2026-11-01 — foundational for AirportSim and WorldForge alike, and the frame every project decision answers to. Its principles are shared across the studio; the systems that embody them are tailored to each game. In its own five parts:

Pillar 1

Mathematical depth

Model the underlying mechanisms, not superficial scripted outcomes. Build interconnected systems whose interactions produce emergent behaviour; make assumptions explicit and validate them; keep every outcome explainable through evidence of the mechanisms beneath it; and balance fidelity, correctness, and performance. Complexity that adds no real depth is avoided — depth is mechanisms, not decoration.

Pillar 2

Simple design, complex systems underneath

The player gets an approachable experience; the sophistication lives underneath it. Depth must never require mandatory micromanagement, and interface complexity is never confused with sophistication — a cluttered screen is not a deep game.

Pillar 3

A fully fledged Development Mode in every game

Every game eventually carries a serious development environment of its own: simulation inspection, time controls, scenario testing, model visualization, causal tracing, reproducible experiments, and performance diagnostics — the tools appropriate to that game's simulation. Architectural preparation for it begins during engine development; the full implementation comes when the game is ready for it, never prematurely. AirportSim's is already built (WP-09, completed 2026-11-03); WorldForge's waits with everything else about that game.

Pillar 4

Codenamed versions, intelligently bumped

Significant releases and major milestones carry codenames — formally designated, recorded in the project's registry, and then stable and unique: never invented, never back-assigned. Version numbers move by the change's actual scope and impact — semantic versioning as a starting point, not a law: a save-format or architecture change does not automatically make a release major. Three identifiers are kept separate, always: the development build's identity, the release version, and the release codename. Publishing or tagging anything requires authorization first.

Pillar 5

Simulation at the center

Behaviour derives from coherent models interacting causally; scripted outcomes are minimised, because emergent behaviour is the goal. Everything else — interface, tooling, process — serves that simulation. Complexity must serve the game: complexity that does not is not depth, it is weight.

Two structural decisions complete the picture. The studio's first games are native Windows products, developed on Linux — and the newest direction reverses the emphasis: LONGSHOT is Linux-first, and the owner's ruling of 2026-11-10 makes AirportSim Linux-only from here on too, played through Ubuntu on WSL; Windows support there is best-effort. A unified game UI — one shared interface across the games — was once planned as a post-release initiative; that plan's world has moved on, and no shared interface work exists or is scheduled. Development Mode remains what it always was: a separate, per-game initiative.

How the studio builds — five engineering principles

The companion document — the engineering philosophy, established the same day — governs how the work is done. Exactly five principles:

1 · Simulation-first engineering

Priorities are established from what the simulation needs; process exists to serve the simulation and expands only where the simulation demonstrably benefits.

2 · Evidence over appearances

A feature is complete when its acceptance criteria are met by verifiable evidence, with the grade named — implemented, demonstrated, or established. Its corollary doctrine: a finding is not a defect — suspicion, characterization, and demonstrated failure are different states of knowledge, and collapsing them corrupts both the record and the fixes.

3 · Causality over superficial functionality

Systems must answer why something happened, traceable to mechanisms and state. A system that produces the right-looking result for the wrong reasons is not working, and is not credited as working.

4 · Autonomous execution with explicit authority

Work proceeds autonomously inside an explicit authorization — and completing work never enlarges that authorization. The boundary moves only when the owner moves it.

5 · One studio methodology, game-specific engineering

Standardize the control plane — evidence standards, records, governance, gates, communication — and specialize the simulation plane: each game's models, architecture, and tools are fitted to its own subject, never forced into another game's shape.
