Mathematics

AirportSim is a mathematics-driven simulation wearing an accessible game’s clothes. The studio’s rule: the mathematics inside is as sophisticated as the subject demands, and the player meets outcomes — a queue that melts away, a storm that snarls the evening bank — never equations. This page shows the mathematics anyway, with the same honesty labels the project’s own records use: what is measured, what is modelled, and what is not yet independently checked.

Determinism comes first

The simulation advances in fixed steps of at most a tenth of a second, and all of its randomness flows from one seeded source whose state lives inside the save file. Same seed, same decisions, same history — bit for bit. That property is not a nicety; it is what makes every other claim on this page testable. A saved game, reloaded, must continue on exactly the trajectory it would have flown anyway, and the test suite holds it to that.

Twice, that property has been broken and hunted down — once by a clock, once by a math library. Both hunts belong on this page, because both were mathematics.

A status changes only when the evidence does — and the registry lives in the game itself, not in a document that can drift away from the code.

The cost of a step

Every step of the simulation is measured, and the measurements are fitted, not admired. The step-cost model (mathematical notebook entry 2026-11-01-A) is T = 10.08 + 0.090·N + 5.36·dt microseconds, where N is the number of active aircraft and dt the step size; its root-mean-square residual against measurement is 0.50 µs. Attribution of the step’s cost: air traffic control ≈ 37.7%, aircraft update 17.0%, roles 14.9%, operations 13.6%, traffic ≈ 8.7%.

Against that model stand two enforced budgets (the GATE-PERF gate): a single step at most 45 µs (measured median 14.1 µs), and a full simulated day at most 1.7 seconds (measured 0.56 s). The budgets were set at three times the measured medians, so a breach means the simulation changed, not that the weather did.

Delay, decomposed

An aircraft’s delay is born at zero when it spawns. It grows only while the aircraft waits — in exactly six defined waiting states — at the simulation’s standard clock rate, from a single site in the code; fixed additions are reserved for defined disruptions, such as an aircraft grounded for repair or a crew problem. Because delay has one birthplace and one growth rule, the instrumented decomposition can account for every minute of it: select the most-delayed aircraft in a running game and Development Mode shows its delay as the sum of its causes. When the parts and the counter disagree, that is a finding, not a rounding error.

Worked case FND-002 — weather belongs to the minute

Until 2026-11-02, a game saved and reloaded mid-minute could continue under different weather than the same game left running — the one property determinism forbids. The cause: the per-minute weather was computed from the fractional clock, so a minute’s weather depended on the sub-minute moment of its first evaluation, which a reload shifts. The fix evaluates each minute’s weather at the minute’s start. A regression test that fails before the fix and passes after it guards the property permanently (issue #15, closed; PR #24). The full hunt is recorded as notebook entry 2026-11-02-A.

Worked case FND-011 — when the platform’s mathematics changed

In late October 2026 the project’s long soak began failing deterministically on newer systems — and only there. The cause, proven by experiment rather than argued: the platform C library’s newest generation returns correctly rounded results for three trigonometric functions, where the older generation was off by one unit in the last place at some inputs. The simulation, tuned for years on the older answers, diverged on day 20 of its reference history and seized entirely on day 368.

The fix states a principle the studio now holds outright: a simulation owns its numerical behaviour wherever that behaviour affects deterministic outcomes. The three functions are implemented in the game itself, bit-for-bit equal to the reference library’s answers — verified exhaustively, not sampled: all 2,131,231,108 float inputs for each of the first two functions and 234,674,762 input pairs for the third, with zero mismatches. With the owned functions in place, the 440-day reference history reproduces digit-for-digit on the new platform. Closed 2026-11-02 (PR #30).

The notebook

Consequential mathematics gets a written record: a mathematical engineering notebook kept with the code, one entry per substantial investigation — the problem, the definitions and assumptions, the derivation, the calculations actually executed (with their outputs), an independent verification, and the conclusions together with what remains uncertain. Two entries stand so far: the step-cost model (2026-11-01-A) and the weather-minute investigation (2026-11-02-A). The notebook supplements the tests and the run records; it never replaces them.

What this page does not claim: that the mathematics is finished, or that every model in the game has earned the trust the checked ones have. The statuses above are the whole point — they are printed so they can improve in public.

Grades and statuses change only on recorded evidence. If this page and the development record disagree, the record is right and this page gets corrected.
